# Procurement gameplay walkthroughs

Revised assistant proposals, November 14, 2026, after the user's correction that this is an accessible game highlighting mission-planning complexity. The scenarios explore decisions, discoveries and spoken coordination. Their enjoyment has not been observed in a playtest.

The previous [state walkthroughs](state-walkthroughs.md) retain technical examples for implementation reference. The scenarios below define the intended player experience for discussion.

## Current scope

D-011 makes ongoing catalogue acquisition and Budget management across many needs the core station activity. The [current gameplay direction](gameplay.md) supersedes these isolated vignettes as the prototype basis. The situations below remain possible moments within that broader play.

## Proposed interaction style

Show what matters for the current decision in ordinary language, with technical properties available as detail. An Offer can communicate cost, arrival relative to need, and demonstrated reliability. Claims such as “proven” need an authored basis consistent with the actual reliability model.

Choosing an Offer opens a short confirmation showing price, delivery, risk and the shared need it will supply. One confirmation could create both the Contract and its Allocation. This is a new interaction proposal. The abstract records remain distinct. The game maintains histories and quantities automatically and makes conflicts legible.

A player learns through an immediate understandable choice, its visible result, and a later complication. Player-facing terminology and controls should support learning by playing. The examples below assume no familiarity with procurement practice.

## 1. We can afford the engine or the ambition

**Situation** — An engine need and a more ambitious camera objective are visible. A proven engine would leave enough money for the basic camera. A cheaper experimental engine could fund the ambitious camera too.

**Player experience** — Inspect a few clearly different Offers. Tell science and planning “We can have the better camera if we accept this engine's risk.” Discuss whether the improved science is worth that risk, then commit to a choice.

**Visible result** — Budget changes, the selected Contract appears, and the chosen need shows reserved supply. The instrument options still affordable remain legible.

**Complexity expressed** — Mission objectives compete for limited funds. Reliability, scientific ambition and cost interact.

**Gameplay validation** — Can a new player explain the dilemma immediately? Do players argue for different priorities? Does each path have an understandable consequence? Does the procurement player have a choice worth bringing to teammates?

## 2. The bargain changes the plan

**Situation** — A supplier offers an attractive engine deal. Its delivery would miss the current need-by date. Other suitable Offers arrive in time but cost more.

**Player experience** — Notice the bargain and ask planning “Can we wait for this? It would leave money for the camera.” Planning knows what moving the schedule would mean and explains that consequence. Procurement can accept the more expensive Offer, negotiate a revised need, or explore another route.

**Visible result** — The accepted schedule and actual Contract terms are recorded. The interface shows whether they fit. Conversation itself changes no money or dates.

**Complexity expressed** — Purchasing decisions and launch preparation schedules interact. A desirable local saving can create a problem elsewhere.

**Gameplay validation** — Does the bargain prompt useful conversation? Can the planner explain why waiting matters? Can players find the relevant dates without learning a scheduling tool?

## 3. Put the problem to the suppliers

**Situation** — Existing Offers cannot satisfy a need comfortably. Procurement opens a Call. After an authored wait, two responses arrive. One is a dependable but expensive adaptation. Another offers a cheaper experimental solution with an explicit compromise.

**Player experience** — Open the Call using the existing shared need. Work on other needs while waiting. Inspect the responses and bring the interesting compromise to teammates.

**Visible result** — Responses are concrete authored Offers. Choosing one creates the usual commitment. The Call requires no player-written procurement document.

**Complexity expressed** — Existing products and custom solutions have different costs, lead times and uncertainty. Procurement can discover new possibilities for the team.

**Gameplay validation** — Is opening a Call an interesting investment of time? Do its responses change the team's options? Can the player stay usefully occupied during the wait? Are the compromises clear and fairly signaled?

## 4. A better idea arrives after commitment

**Situation** — The team already committed to a camera. Science completes research that makes a better model affordable. The improvement is tempting, but replacing the old commitment has a visible cancellation cost and a different delivery date.

**Player experience** — Compare keeping the current plan with switching. Ask science what the improvement enables and ask planning whether it still fits. Confirm a replacement only after seeing the overall cost and timing.

**Visible result** — The old Contract is retained in history or remains usable if delivered. Any replacement uses a new Contract and appropriate Allocations. The game performs the accounting and exposes its consequence.

**Complexity expressed** — Research, timing and prior commitments interact. Better technology can create a decision even after a need seems solved.

**Gameplay validation** — Does the new option feel like an opportunity? Can the player understand the price of changing their mind? Does replacement involve an interesting judgment with a short interaction?

## 5. The delivery warning

**Situation** — A supplier reports an engine delay. Procurement sees a concrete problem and accessible alternatives. The team might accept a revised schedule, pay for a replacement, or pursue an authored communications option that could help.

**Player experience** — Explain the problem aloud. Teammates consider their own available actions while procurement checks the alternatives. Choose a response with understandable cost, time and risk.

**Visible result** — Actual outcomes update dates, funds or access. The player can trace the revised situation to what happened and what the team chose. The supplier's warning and alternatives are authored to keep the decision legible.

**Complexity expressed** — A space mission depends on organizations and schedules beyond the team's immediate control. Resilience costs money, time or flexibility.

**Gameplay validation** — Does the event create a recovery decision players can influence? Is there enough time to respond? Does it prompt coordination while leaving each role meaningful work?

## Earlier proposed vignette sequence

The following narrow sequence predates D-011 and is retained as proposal history. The current validation should exercise the sustained catalogue loop.

Start with scenario 1 so the player learns to compare Offers and make a commitment. Introduce a Call as another option. Later reveal a research opportunity or a supplier complication. These are candidate beats for one handcrafted mission exercise. Their number, pacing and order remain proposals.

Observe decisions and conversation before expanding the catalogue. Record what confused players, which choices they cared about, and whether they wanted to try another approach. State consistency remains necessary for implementation, and the [state walkthroughs](state-walkthroughs.md) can support that work.
