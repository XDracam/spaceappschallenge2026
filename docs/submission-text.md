## Project Name

Mission Control

## High-Level Project Summary

Mission Control is a cooperative browser game about designing and delivering a space mission together. Four players operate Mission Planning, Procurement, Science, and Communications, sharing one budget, schedule, and mission outcome. Players configure spacecraft and trajectories, source equipment, research capabilities, test hardware, and negotiate with stakeholders. Each decision creates consequences for the rest of the team.

Our prototype includes two missions: deploying a geostationary weather satellite and sending an orbiter and lander to Mars. It addresses the Space Mission Design Game challenge by connecting mission design, resource management, and simulation in a shared experience. Our goal is to make the engineering trade-offs and human coordination behind space exploration understandable through play.

## Detailed Project Description

Space missions depend on many specialties working together. A promising instrument still needs power, a suitable trajectory, funding, and delivery before launch. Mission Control turns these interdependencies into a cooperative game.

Players work at four connected stations:

- Mission Planning: Configure the launch vehicle and spacecraft, select equipment, build a sequence of maneuvers, and evaluate whether the mission can meet its objectives.
- Procurement: Compare supplier offers, commission offers for unmet requirements, and manage contracts. Balance cost, delivery time, capability, and reliability against the shared budget and launch schedule.
- Science: Research technologies that expand the team’s engineering capabilities and test delivered hardware to reduce its risk of failure. Choose research paths and priorities around the mission’s needs.
- Communications: Handle stakeholder conversations and pursue projects with government, industry, international partners, and the public. Relationships and negotiations can affect funding, equipment offers, research, and mission events.

All stations act on the same evolving mission. Shared tasks connect requests to the work needed to resolve them, while players can also coordinate through conversation. Our central gameplay goal is to give each role meaningful decisions alongside the others.

For example, a camera requirement can create a research problem for Science, a purchasing and delivery problem for Procurement, and a scheduling decision for Planning. Communications may pursue a partnership that provides another route to the needed capability. The team must decide how to use its available money and time.

The prototype contains two scenarios. The introductory Weather Satellite mission asks players to reach geostationary orbit and return weather imagery. The Mars Orbiter + Lander mission adds interplanetary travel, spacecraft separation, orbital mapping, and surface science. Players prepare their mission against an advancing calendar, then see a goal-by-goal outcome based on the configured plan, acquired hardware, and reliability.

The simulation combines simplified orbital mechanics with checks on fuel, mass, power, data transmission, equipment capability, and timing. Mission content and events are prepared in advance. The game is an educational prototype with deliberate abstractions; its numerical models and fictional suppliers are not intended for operational mission planning.

We want players to experience why a technically attractive design can become difficult to deliver, and how collaboration can reveal alternatives. The intended educational value is learning to connect scientific objectives with engineering, acquisition, risk, and stakeholder decisions. Further human multiplayer playtesting is needed to assess learning outcomes, balance, and how consistently every role remains engaged.

Mission Control is built with TypeScript, Preact, HTML Canvas, and Vite. Browsers share the session through WebRTC using PeerJS for room connections, and the application is hosted on GitHub Pages. ## NASA Data / Resources

We reviewed Space Mission Concept Development Using Concept Maturity Levels by Randii R. Wessen, Chester Borden, John Ziemer, and Johnny Kwok, available through the JPL repository and linked from the challenge resources.

We used the paper as a design reference when examining iterative mission development, parallel work across specialties, acquisition lead times, technology readiness, and trade-offs between scientific return, cost, and risk. Our four stations are a gameplay interpretation; they do not reproduce NASA’s organizational structure or implement the paper’s formal maturity assessment.

This use is conceptual and educational rather than ingestion of a NASA observational dataset. The mission scenarios, supplier offers, costs, research progression, and events are game content.
